# Critical-Site Sensitivity and Realizable Calibration in GCN-to-SNN Conversion

## Abstract

Converting graph convolutional networks (GCNs) at very low temporal budgets couples finite-event approximation with graph-dependent error propagation. We study whether conditional activation-site sensitivity can guide calibration and residual-event selection beyond local reconstruction. Historical three-teacher experiments establish a T1 fidelity gap despite near-teacher accuracy at longer prefixes on MNIST and CIFAR10 superpixel graphs. A continuous causal recovery diagnostic closes 54.08% and 23.54% of teacher-prediction disagreement by recovering the first node site, respectively. Realizable offset, amplitude-alphabet and fixed-opportunity residual-allocation tests then compare actual graph operators with local, feature-only and permuted controls. In single-teacher exploratory screens on 512 validation graphs, actual-message allocation improves immediate message reconstruction but reaches T1 accuracies of 79.49% and 49.22%, against stronger local-fit references of 78.13% and 49.41%; its MNIST accuracy is also close to the permuted selector's 79.30%. At T2 it falls 0.586 percentage points below its unchanged base on both tasks. Instrumented RTX 3060 timing increases relative to local allocation. These results separate conditional sensitivity and message fidelity from demonstrated classification benefit: the tested candidates do not establish a robust graph-specific advantage.

## 1 Introduction

Graph convolution couples representation error to topology. Subsequent propagation mixes a node's activation perturbation with neighboring signals, making its effect on downstream representations depend on both the graph and the perturbation's direction. This raises a specific question for graph conversion: can a calibration objective that accounts for the next message improve on local activation matching?

The question matters especially under low temporal budgets. An SNN with only a few active ticks has limited capacity to represent a continuous activation, while ANN-to-SNN conversion must preserve the downstream computation that gives those activations their utility. Existing approaches address this tension through calibration, alternative temporal codes, and adaptive firing or timestep choices. Yet sensitivity under an idealized continuous perturbation does not guarantee a useful finite-event correction. Coding capacity, the calibration target, and the graph operator can each intervene between sensitivity and classification.

We study this gap in GCN-to-SNN conversion. We test whether actual next-message perturbations provide a better calibration signal than local reconstruction, and whether selecting residual events under a fixed opportunity budget uses graph structure beyond matched controls. Policies are compared within the same graphs, with counterfactual operator controls used to test the contribution of the actual graph mapping. A continuous causal ideal-recovery diagnostic characterizes sensitivity; it is not a deployable coding method. Here, active ticks are simulation steps, an event is a signed graded scalar amplitude, and the opportunity budget bounds extra per-channel event slots rather than equalizing actual energy.

The empirical answer is bounded. In the tested strong-teacher setting, message objectives and fixed-budget residual selection did not establish a robust structural advantage over local and counterfactual controls. At MNIST T2, actual-message allocation was lower than baseline by 0.586 percentage points and lower than local allocation by 0.977 points on paired means over 512 graphs; both descriptive intervals crossed zero and quantify graph sampling only. At T1, message allocation reduced normalized actual next-message MSE relative to local allocation on the reported MNIST and CIFAR10 validations, but this representation-level change does not by itself imply improved classification. Thus, the results distinguish an observable sensitivity or message-matching signal from a demonstrated classification benefit and do not support a claim of a successful new conversion algorithm.

## 2 Related Work

ANN-to-SNN conversion uses calibration data and pretrained ANN activations to reduce mismatches introduced by spiking dynamics. Layerwise calibration and data-driven threshold or initial-potential settings use activation information to configure conversion [li2021, bojkovic2024]. Trainable neuron quantization studies representational limits at low timesteps [atif2023], while differential coding changes how temporal differences encode activations [huang2025]. Other work addresses temporal misalignment [bojkovic2025] or develops coarse-to-fine calibration and coding strategies, including approaches for spiking language models and single-spike conversion [fas2025, onespike2024]. These methods provide distinct ways to manage temporal coding and calibration. They do not resolve whether targeting the actual next graph message improves on matched local and operator controls in the setting studied here.

Another line of work adapts quantization to graph structure. Degree-sensitive masking and aggregation-aware quantization account for node or aggregation properties when selecting quantization behavior [degreequant2021, a2q2023]. Adaptive calibration also considers firing patterns, sensitivity, and input-dependent temporal budgets [adafire2024]. This literature motivates studying graph-dependent errors. Our empirical question is narrower: whether sensitivity-informed allocation of a finite event opportunity budget improves low-timestep converted GCN classification beyond local reconstruction and counterfactual operators. We test this distinction directly, without inferring it from graph-aware quantization results or comparisons across incompatible published benchmarks. The GCN classification setting follows established graph benchmark and superpixel task designs [dwivedi2023].

## 3 Method

[FIGURE:framework]

### 3.1 Teachers and graph conversion

We studied frozen four-layer residual graph convolutional networks (GCNs) for MNIST and CIFAR10 superpixel graph classification. Node features and positions are concatenated and embedded into 146 channels. Each of four node branches computes
\[
H_{\ell+1}=H_\ell+\operatorname{ReLU}(A H_\ell W_\ell^{\mathsf T}+b_\ell),
\]
where evaluation batch normalization is folded into the weights and biases. For every original directed edge $u\to v$, the propagation matrix has $A_{vu}=1/\sqrt{\max(d_{\mathrm{out}}(u),1)\max(d_{\mathrm{in}}(v),1)}$; no additional self-loops are inserted. Mean pooling is followed by linear layers of widths 146, 73, 36, and 10, with ReLU after the two hidden readout layers. The teacher weights remain fixed throughout all conversion and calibration comparisons.

The baseline adapts differential coding [huang2025], replacing each activation site with a differential event unit. There are four node sites and two readout sites. At a site with channel threshold $\theta$, each active tick may emit zero or a signed graded amplitude $\theta 2^{-k}$, for $k=0,\ldots,7$. These are scalar amplitudes rather than binary spikes. At active tick $t$, given incoming drive $c_t$, membrane $v$, cumulative event sum $C$, and auxiliary states $a,b$, the pre-event states are
\[
m=v+c_t+a-b,\qquad \widetilde C=C+b.
\]
A positive event at level $k$ is eligible when $m\geq 0.75\theta 2^{-k}$. Negative emission requires both $m\leq-0.75\theta 2^{-k}$ and $\widetilde C\geq\theta 2^{-k}$. The largest eligible magnitude is selected. After hard event selection $s$, the states update as
\[
v\leftarrow m-s,\quad C\leftarrow\widetilde C+s,\quad a\leftarrow a+c_t/t,\quad b\leftarrow b+s/t.
\]
The decoded activation is $\sum_t s_t/t$. Raw inputs and affine biases are introduced once, at the first active tick. We use $T$ for the number of active ticks.

### 3.2 Calibration objectives and controls

Calibration changes initial membrane offsets while preserving the teacher weights, channel thresholds, and hard event decisions in the forward pass. The common histogram thresholds were estimated on the full training split before these screens. An offset is initialized to zero and parameterized as $\theta\rho$, with $\rho$ clipped to $[-0.5,0.5]$. Fitting all six sites uses 693 offsets; first-site-only fitting uses 146. We used the same 512 fixed-index training calibration graphs, batch size 32, and 64 Adam updates for the screening comparisons, with learning rates 0.001 and 0.01 treated as separate conditions. The backward pass uses a ReLU membrane surrogate; it does not replace hard events in the forward computation.

The all-site local objective averages six sitewise mean-squared errors, each divided by the batchwise teacher activation mean square with denominator floor $10^{-6}$. For first-site message calibration, we average the normalized next-message error and the corresponding local loss. If $E$ is the converted-minus-teacher error at that site, the immediate preactivation perturbation under the fixed folded graph convolution is $A E W_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T}$. Its squared norm is normalized by the mean square of $A H_{\mathrm{teacher}}W_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T}$. This identity isolates the linear propagation contribution: the residual identity path and subsequent nonlinearities remain separate, so the objective is a local proxy rather than an exact final-classification loss. The feature control substitutes the identity for $A$; the structural control uses a fixed within-graph conjugation $P^{-1}AP$. Local, feature-only, permuted, and actual-operator message policies therefore distinguish local reconstruction from dependence on the actual graph mapping.

We also compared fixed source-percentile settings from the differential-coding implementation [huang2025]. The GCN comparator estimates per-channel 0.999 quantiles, floored at $10^{-6}$, from frozen post-ReLU teacher activations on the 512 training graphs. It is an adaptation of the source prescription, not a complete reproduction of its original converter. The original image example uses four levels, threshold scale 4 and 32 ticks. We transfer its level/scale constants to $T=1$ and $T=2$ only as a configuration diagnostic. Its 512-graph threshold estimate also differs from the inherited full-training histogram, so this is not a fully matched reproduction of the source pipeline. A second preset uses eight levels and threshold scale 1 to match the local event alphabet; no validation-based scale search is used.

A separate T1 hard-offset diagnostic uses one sweep over 146 channel offsets and 33 fixed candidates per coordinate on $[-0.5,0.5]$, retaining the incumbent when no hard-loss decrease is found. Its 4,818 candidate evaluations are not budget-matched to 64 Adam updates. This coarse grid can miss small-offset minima and does not establish a global capacity bound.

### 3.3 Continuous causal diagnostic

To measure conditional sensitivity separately from finite-event realizability, we define an ideal recovery intervention on the current converted signal, not on teacher hidden states. For incoming converted increments $c_t$, let $u_t=u_{t-1}+c_t/t$ and emit
\[
d_t=t\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(u_t)-\operatorname{ReLU}(u_{t-1})\bigr).
\]
The weighted prefix $\sum_{t\leq T}d_t/t$ equals $\operatorname{ReLU}(u_T)$. Thus, recovery is causal with respect to the current incoming signal and requires no teacher activation overwrite; at other idealized sites, incoming signals may still differ from the teacher. The empty intervention is the uncalibrated conversion, and replacing all six sites was empirically verified to reproduce the folded ANN. Single-site and grouped effects are conditional and nonadditive. We use this continuous construction only as a diagnostic, not as a realizable SNN coding policy.

### 3.4 First-site alphabet and residual allocation

The alphabet-capacity probe changes only the first site. It replaces the positive amplitude ladder with eight nonnegative channel-specific values plus zero, using nearest-neighbor midpoint decisions while retaining signed differential emissions; all later sites retain their original quantizer. Sixteen weighted Lloyd iterations fit 1,168 scalar values. A separate hard-objective refinement starts from the same local-Lloyd solution and makes one 1,168-coordinate sweep, considering step fractions 1, 0.5, and 0.25; an incumbent is retained unless the actual hard loss decreases. This diagnostic has a larger fitting and storage budget than the 64-step Adam calibration and the 146 first-site offsets. “Full-message” refinement minimizes the complete squared message objective, including cross terms, but still modifies only the first site. Separate six-site local and message membrane probes each use 693 offsets.

The residual-allocation candidate likewise changes only the first site, at its first active tick. At the first site, preactivation $z$ and its current hard event $s$ are already available. We compute $E=s-\operatorname{ReLU}(z)$ from this same incoming converted signal; no teacher hidden activation is queried. Computing that ReLU is an additional analog operation. A candidate correction $c$ is the nearest signed quantization of $-E$ from the eight geometric amplitudes $\theta/(4\cdot 2^k)$, $k=0,\ldots,7$, together with zero. For a candidate at node $i$, the exact single-node reduction in squared next-message error is
\[
-2\left\langle A E W_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T},\; A_{:,i}(c_iW_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T})\right\rangle
-\left\|A_{:,i}(c_iW_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T})\right\|^2.
\]
Local and message gains are normalized by their corresponding graphwise reference mean-square energies (each floored at $10^{-6}$), averaged with equal weight, and ranked once for top-$k$ selection. Candidate interactions make joint gains nonadditive. The selected nodes are the top $\lceil n/4\rceil$ nodes in each graph. Each selected node has up to 146 extra scalar event slots, giving a per-graph opportunity cap of $146\lceil n/4\rceil$ slots. This is an upper bound on extra per-channel events, not an equal-energy constraint: policies can use different numbers of nonzero extras. The extra events update membrane, cumulative output, and decoded output consistently. The same offsets fitted at $T=1$ are used at both $T=1$ and $T=2$.

### 3.5 Evaluation scope

Historical full experiments use teachers with seeds 41, 42, and 43. Official train/validation/test counts are 55,000/5,000/10,000 for MNIST and 45,000/5,000/10,000 for CIFAR10. Their test-set results describe the established baseline and teacher-seed variation; both official validation and test sets had historical exposure. New method screens use teacher 41, 512 training calibration graphs, and a fixed-index validation subset of 512 graphs. Test data were not loaded for selecting the new methods. These comparisons are exploratory; the other 4,488 previously exposed validation graphs do not constitute an independent external set. We report accuracy, agreement with each method's own teacher, and logit mean-squared error where measured. Historical uncertainty is the sample standard deviation across three teacher seeds; new paired intervals describe graph sampling for one teacher only, not seed variation, multiplicity, or external generalization.

## 4 Experiments and Results

### 4.1 Baseline fidelity and conditional sensitivity

Across the historical three-teacher test evaluation, the ANN teachers reached 91.0233% mean accuracy on MNIST (sample SD 0.2650%) and 54.9400% on CIFAR10 (0.0700%). Baseline conversion was strongly timestep-dependent at the shortest prefix: MNIST accuracy was 71.4367% (2.8010%) at $T=1$ and 90.1567% (0.4850%) at $T=2$; CIFAR10 was 46.7967% (1.2240%) and 54.0700% (0.2427%), respectively. At $T=4$ and $T=8$, baseline accuracy approached the corresponding teacher performance on both datasets. All-site activation and graph-logit calibration share the same event code and 693 initial-state offsets; they change the fitting objective rather than the coding scheme. These historical figures are descriptive context from the 10,000 official test graphs per dataset.

The ideal causal diagnostic indicates substantial but site-dependent conditional recoverability at $T=1$. Recovering the first site closed 54.08% of the teacher-prediction disagreement gap on MNIST and 23.54% on CIFAR10; recovery at node sites 0–3 closed 80.42% and 45.18%, while head sites 4–5 closed 8.82% and 14.91%, respectively. Replacing all six sites closed 100% of the gap on both datasets, consistent with the folded-ANN verification. These are conditional continuous interventions, averaged over three teachers on historically exposed validation data; unequal intervention group sizes and nonadditivity preclude interpreting them as additive site contributions or finite-event gains.

### 4.2 Calibration objectives and realizability

The teacher-41 T1 screen separates first-site targeting from the fitting objective (Table \ref{tab:realizability}). With 146 offsets and learning rate 0.001, first-local/message accuracy was 73.63/73.83\% on MNIST and 45.51/43.95\% on CIFAR10. The corresponding 693-offset all-local/message values were 75.00/74.41\% and 47.85/48.83\%. The higher-rate all-local control reached 78.13\% and 49.41\%. Thus first-site sensitivity did not translate into a consistent advantage from first-site-only fitting or the message term. These are single-teacher validation accuracies, not seed averages.

Hard training loss sometimes increased under the surrogate despite finite gradients and actual parameter changes. A bounded hard-offset search was therefore used as an optimization diagnostic; it did not establish a consistent actual-graph advantage over the feature and permuted controls. Its coarse grid and different update budget limit the interpretation: failure of this search is not a proof that the parameter family has no useful solution.

Greater first-site representational flexibility improved MNIST but did not rescue the structural objective. Local/diagonal-message Lloyd alphabets achieved 82.23/82.23\% on MNIST and 48.44/47.46\% on CIFAR10. After one matched hard-coordinate refinement, local/full-message accuracies were 83.20/82.03\% and 47.46/46.09\%. The full-message objective includes cross terms, but all these alphabet interventions remain restricted to the first site. Their 1,168 fitted values and additional optimization work differ from the offset baselines; they are capacity and objective diagnostics, not evidence of superiority under the same fitting budget.

### 4.3 Fixed-opportunity residual allocation

[FIGURE:allocation_contrasts]

Residual allocation was evaluated against baseline, random selection, local-gain selection, identity-operator selection, and shuffled-operator selection, all using the same per-graph opportunity rule. The budget provides 146 extra scalar slots per selected node and $146\lceil n/4\rceil$ per graph. Across the 512-graph validation subset, this totals 1,344,222 slots over 9,207 selected nodes for MNIST and 2,225,186 slots over 15,241 selected nodes for CIFAR10. Actual message-policy nonzero extras were 612,232 and 926,039, respectively; thus equal opportunity did not imply equal realized event counts or energy.

At $T=1$, actual-message allocation reached 79.49% on MNIST and 49.22% on CIFAR10. Relative to baseline, paired mean differences were +4.4922 percentage points on MNIST (descriptive graph-sampling interval 1.1087 to 7.8757) and +1.3672 on CIFAR10 (−1.9932 to 4.7275). Relative to local allocation, differences were +2.3438 points (−0.2472 to 4.9347) and +0.5859 (−2.3569 to 3.5288), respectively. Absolute results for identity and shuffled-operator controls are reported in Table \ref{tab:allocation}; paired contrasts with the controls, including the stronger-local condition, are summarized in Figure \ref{fig:allocation_contrasts}. They do not establish a consistent actual-operator advantage. These paired intervals describe graph sampling for one teacher and do not establish seed-level reliability or generalization.

At $T=2$, message allocation achieved 88.67% on MNIST and 53.32% on CIFAR10, versus baseline values of 89.26% and 53.91%. Message-minus-baseline differences were −0.5859 points on both tasks (MNIST interval −2.2554 to 1.0836; CIFAR10 −2.5012 to 1.3293). Message-minus-local differences were −0.9766 points on MNIST (−2.4582 to 0.5051) and +0.9766 on CIFAR10 (−0.6011 to 2.5542). The direction varies by dataset and comparator; the full set of matched controls is retained in the table.

Actual-message residual allocation reduced mean normalized immediate next-message MSE to 0.001280 on MNIST and 0.001717 on CIFAR10, compared with 0.001378 and 0.002018 for local allocation. Every policy is evaluated under the true operator for this diagnostic. This is an allocation result: it does not show that membrane calibration improved message error, and the reduction did not coincide with robust classification gains.

The practical expansion rule required at least 0.5 percentage points over local selection and the stronger local accuracy reference on both tasks, superiority to feature/permuted controls, and at most 0.5 points of T2 regression relative to the identical frozen T1 base. Neither task passed the joint rule. This is an exploratory selection criterion, not a significance test; the failed screen did not trigger additional teacher or backbone expansion.


### 4.4 Source adaptation and diagnostic cost

The source-percentile transfer exposed a configuration-sensitive adverse case: the four-level, threshold-scale-4 image preset produced very low $T=1$ accuracy on both tasks and remained weak at $T=2$. With the eight-level, scale-1 event-matched preset, CIFAR10 reached 46.29% at $T=1$ and 55.08% at $T=2$; MNIST reached 71.88% and 88.09%. This supports treating the transferred image configuration as a diagnostic rather than attributing its performance to graph structure. Alphabet probes likewise have a different resource scope from the low-dimensional offset fits: first-site Lloyd fitting stores 1,168 scalars, and the optional hard-objective sweep adds a full coordinate pass; neither is an all-site alphabet intervention. The measured evidence therefore does not isolate a structural gain at matched capacity across these separate probes.

The allocation path also added diagnostic runtime in the measured implementation. On an RTX 3060 with cached batches of 32, median synchronized host time per graph was 0.6678 ms for MNIST local allocation and 0.8343 ms for message allocation; for CIFAR10 it was 0.8063 and 0.9914 ms. These medians used 20 randomized-interleaved repeats after five warmups; repeat variation is retained in raw records, with no population confidence interval. Timing includes policy instrumentation and excludes teacher forward and data transfer. It is neither an energy measurement nor an optimized deployment bound.

## 5 Discussion

The results separate sensitivity to graph propagation from the ability to realize a useful correction with a finite event code. Under the fixed folded graph convolution, the next-message perturbation is exactly the graph operator applied to the activation error and then transformed by the next-layer weights. This makes message error a well-defined local target for testing directional error. The continuous causal diagnostic shows that some conversion errors are conditionally recoverable, particularly at node sites, but that intervention can reproduce a continuous ReLU response and does not impose the finite alphabet, event dynamics, or downstream classification constraints of the converted network. It therefore establishes a sensitivity property of the studied computation, not an available low-timestep coding policy.

The membrane-calibration screen did not establish a consistent advantage for the actual-message objective over local fitting. Separately, actual-message residual allocation reduced its targeted immediate message error, but this did not produce robust classification gains. The message objective covers the immediate linear propagation term; it is a proxy for that term, not an exact loss for the residual block or final prediction. Subsequent nonlinearities and errors at other sites remain in the computation. These results are consistent with a mismatch between local message fidelity and classification, but they do not identify which downstream component accounts for the observed differences.

The fixed-opportunity allocation experiment provides a direct test of whether using the actual graph mapping adds value beyond selecting residual corrections. At the shortest prefix, message allocation sometimes improved on baseline, most clearly for MNIST, but its paired comparisons with local and counterfactual operator controls were inconclusive across the two datasets. At two active ticks, the direction of the differences varied by dataset and comparator, with the reported graph-sampling intervals crossing zero. Thus, the observed short-prefix gains do not establish a robust contribution from the actual graph structure. The controls are informative because a ranking based on the actual operator can appear graph-aware while gains may also arise from residual selection or the allocation procedure itself. In this study, the comparisons do not distinguish a dependable actual-operator advantage from those alternatives.

The opportunity budget also limits what can be inferred about resource efficiency. It bounds extra per-channel event slots but does not equalize the number of nonzero corrections or their energy across policies. Consequently, accuracy comparisons under this rule assess policies with matched opportunities, not matched realized activity or energy. The measured allocation path incurred additional host time under the reported implementation and timing boundary. This is a diagnostic cost for the tested procedure; it does not establish an optimized deployment cost, hardware energy, or a general runtime penalty for graph-aware methods.

Capacity and configuration further constrain interpretation. The adverse transferred source-percentile configuration shows that a preset developed for another setting need not be suitable for these graph models. The event-matched preset and the larger first-site alphabet probes use different coding or fitting resources from the offset calibrations, so their results do not isolate a structural effect at matched capacity. More broadly, the experiments concern one strong teacher for the new method screens and a fixed subset of previously exposed validation data; the paired intervals describe graph sampling for that teacher, not seed variation or external generalization. The evidence supports a bounded conclusion about these tested methods and conditions, while leaving their reliability across teachers and independently sampled data unresolved.

## 6 Conclusion

In the tested low-timestep GCN-to-SNN setting, critical-site sensitivity did not translate into an established, realizable graph-specific classification advantage. Actual-message residual allocation improved its targeted immediate message error, but neither the membrane-calibration screen nor fixed-opportunity allocation established robust classification superiority over local and counterfactual controls across datasets and active-tick settings. Continuous ideal recovery identifies conditional sensitivity, while finite-event calibration and allocation remain constrained by coding capacity and by the fact that immediate message error is only a proxy for final prediction. These findings do not support a claim of a successful new conversion method; they show that sensitivity and message matching alone are insufficient evidence for a practical structural benefit in the evaluated setting.
