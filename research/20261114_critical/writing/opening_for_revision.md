# Critical-Site Sensitivity and Realizable Calibration in GCN-to-SNN Conversion

## Abstract
Low-timestep conversion of graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to spiking neural networks (SNNs) is constrained by both temporal coding capacity and graph propagation: a local activation error can be amplified, attenuated, or redirected by the next graph operator. We ask whether identifying critical sites and optimizing the resulting message perturbations yields graph-specific classification gains beyond local calibration. In a bounded study with strong pretrained GCN teachers, we compare actual next-message objectives and fixed-opportunity-budget residual allocation with local and within-graph counterfactual controls, and use continuous ideal recovery only as a sensitivity diagnostic. The diagnostics distinguish sensitivity from realizability, but the tested message objectives and allocation policy do not establish a robust structural advantage over matched controls. For example, on MNIST at two active ticks, message-based allocation differed from baseline by -0.586 accuracy percentage points (paired mean over 512 graphs; descriptive graph-sampling interval, -2.255 to 1.084), and from local allocation by -0.977 points (-2.458 to 0.505). These exploratory, single-teacher comparisons do not establish equivalence or generalization. The evidence therefore motivates treating critical-site sensitivity as a design signal, not as evidence that a graph-aware calibration will improve low-timestep conversion.

## 1 Introduction
Graph convolution couples representation error to topology. An activation perturbation at one node is mixed with neighboring signals by subsequent propagation, so its effect on a downstream representation depends on the graph and on the direction of the perturbation. This makes graph conversion a natural setting in which to ask whether a calibration objective that accounts for the next message can improve on matching activations locally.

The question is practically important at low temporal budgets. An SNN operating for only a few active ticks has limited opportunity to represent a continuous activation, while ANN-to-SNN conversion must also preserve the downstream computation that makes those activations useful. Existing conversion approaches address this tension through calibration, alternative temporal codes, and adaptive firing or timestep choices. Yet a site that is sensitive under an idealized continuous perturbation need not admit a useful finite-event correction: coding capacity, the calibration target, and the graph operator can each intervene between sensitivity and classification.

We investigate that gap in GCN-to-SNN conversion. The study tests whether actual next-message perturbations provide a better calibration signal than local reconstruction, and whether selecting residual events under a fixed opportunity budget exploits graph structure beyond matched controls. We compare policies within the same graphs and include counterfactual operator controls to isolate the contribution of the actual graph mapping. A continuous causal ideal-recovery diagnostic characterizes sensitivity; it is not treated as a deployable coding method. Active ticks denote the number of simulation steps, an event is a signed graded scalar amplitude, and the opportunity budget is an upper bound on extra per-channel events rather than a measure of equal energy.

The empirical answer is bounded. In the tested strong-teacher setting, message objectives and fixed-budget residual selection did not establish a robust structural advantage over local and counterfactual controls. At MNIST T2, actual-message allocation was lower than baseline by 0.586 percentage points and lower than local allocation by 0.977 points on paired 512-graph means; the corresponding descriptive intervals crossed zero and quantify graph sampling only. At T1, message allocation reduced normalized actual next-message MSE relative to local allocation on the reported MNIST and CIFAR10 validations, but that representation-level change does not by itself imply classification improvement. The results separate an observable sensitivity or message-matching signal from a demonstrated classification benefit, and do not support a claim of a successful new conversion algorithm.

## 2 Related Work
ANN-to-SNN conversion has used calibration data and pretrained ANN activations to reduce the mismatch introduced by spiking dynamics. Layerwise calibration and data-driven threshold or potential initialization use activation information to set conversion parameters [li2021, bojkovic2024]. Work on trainable neuron quantization studies low-timestep representational limits [atif2023], while differential coding changes how temporal differences encode activations [huang2025]. Other approaches address temporal alignment directly [bojkovic2025] or use coarse-to-fine calibration and coding strategies, including work on spiking language models and single-spike conversion [fas2025, onespike2024]. These methods establish several ways to manage temporal coding and calibration; they do not answer whether targeting the actual next graph message yields gains over matched local and operator controls in the setting studied here.

A complementary literature adapts quantization to graph structure. Degree-sensitive masking and aggregation-aware quantization account for node or aggregation properties when choosing quantization behavior [degreequant2021, a2q2023]. Adaptive calibration also considers firing patterns, sensitivity, and input-dependent temporal budgets [adafire2024]. Such work motivates examining graph-dependent errors, but the empirical question here is narrower: whether a sensitivity-informed, finite opportunity allocation improves low-timestep converted GCN classification beyond local reconstruction and counterfactual operators. We test that distinction directly rather than infer it from graph-aware quantization results or comparisons across incompatible published benchmarks. The GCN classification context follows established graph benchmark and superpixel task designs [dwivedi2023].
