# Critical-Site Sensitivity and Realizable Calibration in GCN-to-SNN Conversion

## Abstract

Low-timestep conversion of graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to spiking neural networks (SNNs) is constrained by temporal coding capacity and graph propagation: a local activation error can be amplified, attenuated, or redirected by the next graph operator. We ask whether identifying critical sites and optimizing their resulting message perturbations yields graph-specific classification gains beyond local calibration. In a bounded study with strong pretrained GCN teachers, we compare objectives based on actual next-message perturbations and fixed-opportunity-budget residual allocation against local and within-graph counterfactual controls. Continuous ideal recovery is used only as a sensitivity diagnostic. These diagnostics separate sensitivity from realizability, but the tested message objectives and allocation policy do not establish a robust structural advantage over matched controls. On MNIST at two active ticks, for example, message-based allocation differed from baseline by -0.586 accuracy percentage points (paired mean over 512 graphs; descriptive graph-sampling interval, -2.255 to 1.084) and from local allocation by -0.977 points (-2.458 to 0.505). These exploratory comparisons use a single teacher and establish neither equivalence nor generalization. The results therefore support treating critical-site sensitivity as a design signal, not as evidence that graph-aware calibration improves low-timestep conversion.

## 1 Introduction

Graph convolution couples representation error to topology. Subsequent propagation mixes a node's activation perturbation with neighboring signals, making its effect on downstream representations depend on both the graph and the perturbation's direction. This raises a specific question for graph conversion: can a calibration objective that accounts for the next message improve on local activation matching?

The question matters especially under low temporal budgets. An SNN with only a few active ticks has limited capacity to represent a continuous activation, while ANN-to-SNN conversion must preserve the downstream computation that gives those activations their utility. Existing approaches address this tension through calibration, alternative temporal codes, and adaptive firing or timestep choices. Yet sensitivity under an idealized continuous perturbation does not guarantee a useful finite-event correction. Coding capacity, the calibration target, and the graph operator can each intervene between sensitivity and classification.

We study this gap in GCN-to-SNN conversion. We test whether actual next-message perturbations provide a better calibration signal than local reconstruction, and whether selecting residual events under a fixed opportunity budget uses graph structure beyond matched controls. Policies are compared within the same graphs, with counterfactual operator controls used to test the contribution of the actual graph mapping. A continuous causal ideal-recovery diagnostic characterizes sensitivity; it is not a deployable coding method. Here, active ticks are simulation steps, an event is a signed graded scalar amplitude, and the opportunity budget bounds extra per-channel event slots rather than equalizing actual energy.

The empirical answer is bounded. In the tested strong-teacher setting, message objectives and fixed-budget residual selection did not establish a robust structural advantage over local and counterfactual controls. At MNIST T2, actual-message allocation was lower than baseline by 0.586 percentage points and lower than local allocation by 0.977 points on paired means over 512 graphs; both descriptive intervals crossed zero and quantify graph sampling only. At T1, message allocation reduced normalized actual next-message MSE relative to local allocation on the reported MNIST and CIFAR10 validations, but this representation-level change does not by itself imply improved classification. Thus, the results distinguish an observable sensitivity or message-matching signal from a demonstrated classification benefit and do not support a claim of a successful new conversion algorithm.

## 2 Related Work

ANN-to-SNN conversion uses calibration data and pretrained ANN activations to reduce mismatches introduced by spiking dynamics. Layerwise calibration and data-driven threshold or initial-potential settings use activation information to configure conversion [li2021, bojkovic2024]. Trainable neuron quantization studies representational limits at low timesteps [atif2023], while differential coding changes how temporal differences encode activations [huang2025]. Other work addresses temporal misalignment [bojkovic2025] or develops coarse-to-fine calibration and coding strategies, including approaches for spiking language models and single-spike conversion [fas2025, onespike2024]. These methods provide distinct ways to manage temporal coding and calibration. They do not resolve whether targeting the actual next graph message improves on matched local and operator controls in the setting studied here.

Another line of work adapts quantization to graph structure. Degree-sensitive masking and aggregation-aware quantization account for node or aggregation properties when selecting quantization behavior [degreequant2021, a2q2023]. Adaptive calibration also considers firing patterns, sensitivity, and input-dependent temporal budgets [adafire2024]. This literature motivates studying graph-dependent errors. Our empirical question is narrower: whether sensitivity-informed allocation of a finite event opportunity budget improves low-timestep converted GCN classification beyond local reconstruction and counterfactual operators. We test this distinction directly, without inferring it from graph-aware quantization results or comparisons across incompatible published benchmarks. The GCN classification setting follows established graph benchmark and superpixel task designs [dwivedi2023].

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Teachers and graph conversion

We studied frozen four-layer residual graph convolutional networks (GCNs) for MNIST and CIFAR10 superpixel graph classification. Node features and positions are concatenated and embedded into 146 channels. Each of four node branches computes
\[
H_{\ell+1}=H_\ell+\operatorname{ReLU}(A H_\ell W_\ell^{\mathsf T}+b_\ell),
\]
where evaluation batch normalization is folded into the weights and biases. For every original directed edge $u\to v$, the propagation matrix has $A_{vu}=1/\sqrt{\max(d_{\mathrm{out}}(u),1)\max(d_{\mathrm{in}}(v),1)}$; no additional self-loops are inserted. Mean pooling is followed by linear layers of widths 146, 73, 36, and 10, with ReLU after the two hidden readout layers. The teacher weights remain fixed throughout all conversion and calibration comparisons.

The baseline replaces each activation site with a differential event unit. There are four node sites and two readout sites. At a site with channel threshold $\theta$, each active tick may emit zero or a signed graded amplitude $\theta 2^{-k}$, for $k=0,\ldots,7$. These are scalar amplitudes rather than binary spikes. At active tick $t$, given incoming drive $c_t$, membrane $v$, cumulative event sum $C$, and auxiliary states $a,b$, the pre-event states are
\[
m=v+c_t+a-b,\qquad \widetilde C=C+b.
\]
A positive event at level $k$ is eligible when $m\geq 0.75\theta 2^{-k}$; a negative event also requires $\widetilde C\geq\theta 2^{-k}$. After hard event selection $s$, the states update as
\[
v\leftarrow m-s,\quad C\leftarrow\widetilde C+s,\quad a\leftarrow a+c_t/t,\quad b\leftarrow b+s/t.
\]
The decoded activation is $\sum_t s_t/t$. Raw inputs and affine biases are introduced once, at the first active tick. We use $T$ for the number of active ticks.

### 3.2 Calibration objectives and controls

Calibration changes initial membrane offsets while preserving the teacher weights, channel thresholds, and hard event decisions in the forward pass. An offset is initialized to zero and parameterized as $\theta\rho$, with $\rho$ clipped to $[-0.5,0.5]$. Fitting all six sites uses 693 offsets; first-site-only fitting uses 146. We used the same 512 fixed-index training calibration graphs, batch size 32, and 64 Adam updates for the screening comparisons, with learning rates 0.001 and 0.01 treated as separate conditions. The backward pass uses a ReLU membrane surrogate; it does not replace hard events in the forward computation.

The all-site local objective averages six sitewise mean-squared errors, each divided by the batchwise teacher activation mean square with denominator floor $10^{-6}$. For first-site message calibration, we add the normalized next-message error to the corresponding local loss. If $E$ is the converted-minus-teacher error at that site, the immediate preactivation perturbation under the fixed folded graph convolution is $A E W_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T}$. Its squared norm is normalized by the mean square of $A H_{\mathrm{teacher}}W_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T}$. This identity isolates the linear propagation contribution: the residual identity path and subsequent nonlinearities remain separate, so the objective is a local proxy rather than an exact final-classification loss. The feature control substitutes the identity for $A$; the structural control uses a fixed within-graph conjugation $P^{-1}AP$. Local, feature-only, permuted, and actual-operator message policies therefore distinguish local reconstruction from dependence on the actual graph mapping.

We also compared fixed source-percentile settings. The GCN comparator estimates per-channel 0.999 quantiles, floored at $10^{-6}$, from frozen post-ReLU teacher activations on the 512 training graphs. It is an adaptation of the source prescription, not a complete reproduction of its original converter. Its four-level, threshold-scale-4, 32-tick image configuration is included only as a transferred-configuration diagnostic at $T=1$ and $T=2$. A second preset uses eight levels and threshold scale 1 to match the local event alphabet; no validation-based scale search is used.

### 3.3 Continuous causal diagnostic

To measure conditional sensitivity separately from finite-event realizability, we define an ideal recovery intervention on the current converted signal, not on teacher hidden states. For incoming converted increments $c_t$, let $u_t=u_{t-1}+c_t/t$ and emit
\[
d_t=t\bigl(\operatorname{ReLU}(u_t)-\operatorname{ReLU}(u_{t-1})\bigr).
\]
The weighted prefix $\sum_{t\leq T}d_t/t$ equals $\operatorname{ReLU}(u_T)$. Thus, recovery is causal with respect to the current incoming signal and requires no teacher activation overwrite; at other idealized sites, incoming signals may still differ from the teacher. The empty intervention is the uncalibrated conversion, and replacing all six sites was empirically verified to reproduce the folded ANN. Single-site and grouped effects are conditional and nonadditive. We use this continuous construction only as a diagnostic, not as a realizable SNN coding policy.

### 3.4 First-site alphabet and residual allocation

The alphabet-capacity probe changes only the first site. It replaces its eight signed levels with eight nonnegative channel-specific values plus zero, using nearest-neighbor midpoint decisions; all later sites retain their original quantizer. Sixteen weighted Lloyd iterations fit 1,168 scalar values. A separate hard-objective refinement starts from the same local-Lloyd solution and makes one 1,168-coordinate sweep, considering step fractions 1, 0.5, and 0.25; an incumbent is retained unless the actual hard loss decreases. This diagnostic has a larger fitting and storage budget than the 64-step Adam calibration and the 146 first-site offsets. “Full-message” refinement minimizes the complete squared message objective, including cross terms, but still modifies only the first site. Separate six-site local and message membrane probes each use 693 offsets.

The residual-allocation candidate likewise changes only the first site, at its first active tick. For each node-channel component, the current decoded activation $z$ is available; with teacher activation $s$, we set $E=s-\operatorname{ReLU}(z)$. A candidate correction $c$ is the nearest signed quantization of $-E$ from the eight geometric amplitudes $\theta/(4\cdot 2^k)$, $k=0,\ldots,7$, together with zero. For a candidate at node $i$, the exact single-node reduction in squared next-message error is
\[
-2\left\langle A E W_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T},\; A_{:,i}(c_iW_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T})\right\rangle
-\left\|A_{:,i}(c_iW_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T})\right\|^2.
\]
Local and message gains are normalized by graphwise teacher energy, averaged equally across the two datasets' graph units, and ranked once for top-$k$ selection. Candidate interactions make joint gains nonadditive. The selected nodes are the top $\lceil n/4\rceil$ nodes in each graph. Each selected node has up to 146 extra scalar event slots, giving a per-graph opportunity cap of $146\lceil n/4\rceil$ slots. This is an upper bound on extra per-channel events, not an equal-energy constraint: policies can use different numbers of nonzero extras. The extra events update membrane, cumulative output, and decoded output consistently. The same offsets fitted at $T=1$ are used at both $T=1$ and $T=2$.

### 3.5 Evaluation scope

Historical full experiments use teachers with seeds 41, 42, and 43. Official train/validation/test counts are 55,000/5,000/10,000 for MNIST and 45,000/5,000/10,000 for CIFAR10. Their test-set results describe the established baseline and teacher-seed variation; both official validation and test sets had historical exposure. New method screens use teacher 41, 512 training calibration graphs, and a fixed-index validation subset of 512 graphs. Test data were not loaded for selecting the new methods. The resulting comparisons are exploratory, not independent external confirmation; the 4,488 other previously exposed validation graphs do not constitute an independent external set. We report accuracy, agreement with each method's own teacher, and logit mean-squared error where measured. Historical uncertainty is the sample standard deviation across three teacher seeds; new paired intervals describe graph sampling for one teacher only, not seed variation, multiplicity, or external generalization.

## 4 Experiments and Results

### 4.1 Baseline fidelity and conditional sensitivity

Across the historical three-teacher test evaluation, the ANN teachers reached 91.0233% mean accuracy on MNIST (sample SD 0.2650%) and 54.9400% on CIFAR10 (0.0700%). Baseline conversion was strongly timestep-dependent at the shortest prefix: MNIST accuracy was 71.4367% (2.8010%) at $T=1$ and 90.1567% (0.4850%) at $T=2$; CIFAR10 was 46.7967% (1.2240%) and 54.0700% (0.2427%), respectively. At $T=4$ and $T=8$, baseline accuracy approached the corresponding teacher performance on both datasets. Node-only and graph-only event variants provide additional coding controls; neither is pooled with the baseline condition. These historical figures are descriptive context from the 10,000 official test graphs per dataset and were not used to select the later methods.

The ideal causal diagnostic indicates substantial but site-dependent conditional recoverability at $T=1$. Recovering the first site closed 54.08% of the teacher-prediction disagreement gap on MNIST and 23.54% on CIFAR10; recovery at node sites 0–3 closed 80.42% and 45.18%, while head sites 4–5 closed 8.82% and 14.91%, respectively. Replacing all six sites closed 100% of the gap on both datasets, consistent with the folded-ANN verification. These are conditional continuous interventions, averaged over three teachers on historically exposed validation data; unequal intervention group sizes and nonadditivity preclude interpreting them as additive site contributions or finite-event gains.

### 4.2 Calibration objectives and realizability

In the teacher-41 screening evaluations on 512 validation graphs, first-site message calibration lowered normalized actual next-message MSE relative to first-site local calibration on both datasets. On MNIST the mean fell from 0.001378 to 0.001280; on CIFAR10 it fell from 0.002018 to 0.001717. These errors were evaluated under the actual normalized $A E W_{\mathrm{next}}^{\mathsf T}$ operator for every policy, over 512 graphs, without seed-level intervals. This confirms that the message target changed the measured representation error in its intended direction, but not that it improved classification.

Classification comparisons were less consistent. On the exploratory MNIST screen, first-site message calibration achieved 82.0313% at $T=1$ versus 83.2031% for first-site local calibration; the all-site local and all-site message conditions reached 83.2031% and 82.0313%, respectively. On CIFAR10, first-site message calibration reached 43.9453%, below first-site local (45.5078%), while the separate six-site local and message conditions reached 47.8516% and 48.8281%. These conditions differ in which sites are fitted and are not matched in parameter count. Screening at learning rates 0.001 and 0.01 is reported as separate optimization conditions: the higher-rate strong-local control is retained in the comparison because allocation gains could otherwise reflect calibration strength rather than graph structure. No single learning rate or objective consistently converted lower message error into higher accuracy across both tasks.

### 4.3 Fixed-opportunity residual allocation

Residual allocation was evaluated against baseline, random selection, local-gain selection, identity-operator selection, and shuffled-operator selection, all using the same per-graph opportunity rule. The budget provides 146 extra scalar slots per selected node and $146\lceil n/4\rceil$ per graph. Across the 512-graph validation subset, this totals 1,344,222 slots over 9,207 selected nodes for MNIST and 2,225,186 slots over 15,241 selected nodes for CIFAR10. Actual message-policy nonzero extras were 612,232 and 926,039, respectively; thus equal opportunity did not imply equal realized event counts or energy.

At $T=1$, actual-message allocation reached 79.492188% on MNIST and 49.218750% on CIFAR10. The paired accuracy difference versus baseline was +4.4922 percentage points on MNIST (descriptive graph-sampling interval 1.1087 to 7.8757) and +1.3672 points on CIFAR10 (−1.9932 to 4.7275). Against local allocation, the corresponding differences were +2.3438 points (−0.2472 to 4.9347) and +0.5859 points (−2.3569 to 3.5288). Against identity controls, they were +2.7344 (−0.2243 to 5.6930) and 0.0000 (−2.9183 to 2.9183); against shuffled controls, +0.1953 (−2.4316 to 2.8223) and +1.9531 (−0.9602 to 4.8665). The MNIST comparison with a stronger local-learning-rate condition was +1.3672 points (−1.9046 to 4.6390), while the CIFAR10 comparison was −0.1953 (−3.6440 to 3.2533). All intervals are paired, descriptive intervals over graphs for one teacher and cross zero except the MNIST allocation-versus-baseline comparison; they do not establish seed-level reliability or a general structural benefit.

At $T=2$, the absolute accuracies were 88.671875% for message allocation, 89.257812% for baseline, 89.648438% for local allocation, 89.453125% for identity, and 89.453125% for shuffled selection on MNIST. On CIFAR10, the corresponding values were 53.320312%, 53.906250%, 52.343750%, 53.125000%, and 52.734375%. The paired message-minus-baseline difference was −0.5859 points on MNIST (−2.2554 to 1.0836) and −0.5859 on CIFAR10 (−2.5012 to 1.3293); message-minus-local was −0.9766 (−2.4582 to 0.5051) on MNIST and +0.9766 (−0.6011 to 2.5542) on CIFAR10. The corresponding differences versus identity and shuffled selection were −0.7812 and −0.7812 points on MNIST, and +0.1953 and +0.5859 on CIFAR10; each interval crossed zero. The direction changes across datasets and controls, and these single-teacher comparisons do not show robust structural superiority.

### 4.4 Capacity and diagnostic cost

The source-percentile transfer exposed a configuration-sensitive adverse case: the four-level, threshold-scale-4 image preset produced very low $T=1$ accuracy on both tasks and remained weak at $T=2$. With the eight-level, scale-1 event-matched preset, CIFAR10 reached 46.289062% at $T=1$ and 55.078125% at $T=2$; MNIST reached 71.875000% and 88.085938%. This supports treating the transferred image configuration as a diagnostic rather than attributing its performance to graph structure. Alphabet probes likewise have a different resource scope from the low-dimensional offset fits: first-site Lloyd fitting stores 1,168 scalars, and the optional hard-objective sweep adds a full coordinate pass; neither is an all-site alphabet intervention. The measured evidence therefore does not isolate a structural gain at matched capacity across these separate probes.

The allocation path also added diagnostic runtime in the measured implementation. On an RTX 3060 with cached batches of 32, median synchronized host time per graph was 0.6678 ms for MNIST local allocation and 0.8343 ms for message allocation; for CIFAR10 it was 0.8063 and 0.9914 ms. These medians used 20 randomized-interleaved repeats after five warmups; repeat variation is retained in raw records, with no population confidence interval. Timing includes policy instrumentation and excludes teacher forward and data transfer. It is neither an energy measurement nor an optimized deployment bound.

## 5 Discussion

The results separate sensitivity to graph propagation from the ability to realize a useful correction with a finite event code. Under the fixed folded graph convolution, the next-message perturbation is exactly the graph operator applied to the activation error and then transformed by the next-layer weights. This makes message error a well-defined local target for testing directional error. The continuous causal diagnostic shows that some conversion errors are conditionally recoverable, particularly at node sites, but that intervention can reproduce a continuous ReLU response and does not impose the finite alphabet, event dynamics, or downstream classification constraints of the converted network. It therefore establishes a sensitivity property of the studied computation, not an available low-timestep coding policy.

The calibration results illustrate the same distinction at the representation level. Optimizing the actual next-message objective reduced its measured message error relative to local reconstruction, yet this change did not produce a consistent accuracy advantage. The objective covers the immediate linear propagation term; it is a proxy for the contribution of that term, not an exact loss for the residual block or final prediction. Subsequent nonlinearities and errors at other sites remain in the computation. Moreover, the converted response is constrained by the signed graded event alphabet and hard event decisions. These observations are consistent with a target mismatch between local message fidelity and classification, but they do not identify which downstream component accounts for the observed differences.

The fixed-opportunity allocation experiment provides a direct test of whether using the actual graph mapping adds value beyond selecting residual corrections. At the shortest prefix, message allocation sometimes improved on baseline, most clearly for MNIST, but its paired comparisons with local and counterfactual operator controls were inconclusive across the two datasets. At two active ticks, the direction of the differences varied by dataset and comparator, with the reported graph-sampling intervals crossing zero. Thus, the observed short-prefix gains do not establish a robust contribution from the actual graph structure. The controls are informative because a ranking based on the actual operator can appear graph-aware while gains may also arise from residual selection or the allocation procedure itself. In this study, the comparisons do not distinguish a dependable actual-operator advantage from those alternatives.

The opportunity budget also limits what can be inferred about resource efficiency. It bounds extra per-channel event slots but does not equalize the number of nonzero corrections or their energy across policies. Consequently, accuracy comparisons under this rule assess policies with matched opportunities, not matched realized activity or energy. The measured allocation path incurred additional host time under the reported implementation and timing boundary. This is a diagnostic cost for the tested procedure; it does not establish an optimized deployment cost, hardware energy, or a general runtime penalty for graph-aware methods.

Capacity and configuration further constrain interpretation. The adverse transferred source-percentile configuration shows that a preset developed for another setting need not be suitable for these graph models. The event-matched preset and the larger first-site alphabet probes use different coding or fitting resources from the offset calibrations, so their results do not isolate a structural effect at matched capacity. More broadly, the experiments concern one strong teacher for the new method screens and a fixed subset of previously exposed validation data; the paired intervals describe graph sampling for that teacher, not seed variation or external generalization. The evidence supports a bounded conclusion about these tested methods and conditions, while leaving their reliability across teachers and independently sampled data unresolved.

## 6 Conclusion

In the tested low-timestep GCN-to-SNN setting, critical-site sensitivity did not translate into an established, realizable graph-specific classification advantage. Actual-message calibration improved its targeted representation error, but fixed-opportunity residual allocation did not show robust superiority over local and counterfactual controls across datasets and active-tick settings. Continuous ideal recovery identifies conditional sensitivity, while finite-event calibration and allocation remain constrained by coding capacity and by the fact that immediate message error is only a proxy for final prediction. These findings do not support a claim of a successful new conversion method; they show that sensitivity and message matching alone are insufficient evidence for a practical structural benefit in the evaluated setting.
