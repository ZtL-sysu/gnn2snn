Critical-Site Sensitivity and Realizable Calibration in GCN-to-SNN
Conversion
Abstract
Converting graph convolutional networks (GCNs) at very low temporal budgets couples finite-event
approximation with graph-dependent error propagation. We study whether conditional activation-site
sensitivity can guide calibration and residual-event selection beyond local reconstruction. Historical
three-teacher experiments establish a T1 fidelity gap despite near-teacher accuracy at longer prefixes on
MNIST and CIFAR10 superpixel graphs. A continuous causal recovery diagnostic closes 54.08% and
23.54% of teacher-prediction disagreement by recovering the first node site, respectively. Realizable offset,
amplitude-alphabet and fixed-opportunity residual-allocation tests then compare actual graph operators
with local, feature-only and permuted controls. In single-teacher exploratory screens on 512 validation
graphs, actual-message allocation improves immediate message reconstruction but reaches T1 accuracies
of 79.49% and 49.22%, against stronger local-fit references of 78.13% and 49.41%; its MNIST accuracy is
also close to the permuted selector’s 79.30%. At T2 it falls 0.586 percentage points below its unchanged
base on both tasks. Instrumented RTX 3060 timing increases relative to local allocation. These results
separate conditional sensitivity and message fidelity from demonstrated classification benefit: the tested
candidates do not establish a robust graph-specific advantage.
1
Introduction
Graph convolution couples representation error to topology. Subsequent propagation mixes a node’s
activation perturbation with neighboring signals, making its effect on downstream representations depend
on both the graph and the perturbation’s direction. This raises a specific question for graph conversion:
can a calibration objective that accounts for the next message improve on local activation matching?
The question matters especially under low temporal budgets. An SNN with only a few active ticks
has limited capacity to represent a continuous activation, while ANN-to-SNN conversion must preserve
the downstream computation that gives those activations their utility. Existing approaches address this
tension through calibration, alternative temporal codes, and adaptive firing or timestep choices. Yet
sensitivity under an idealized continuous perturbation does not guarantee a useful finite-event correction.
Coding capacity, the calibration target, and the graph operator can each intervene between sensitivity and
classification.
We study this gap in GCN-to-SNN conversion. We test whether actual next-message perturbations
provide a better calibration signal than local reconstruction, and whether selecting residual events under
a fixed opportunity budget uses graph structure beyond matched controls. Policies are compared within
the same graphs, with counterfactual operator controls used to test the contribution of the actual graph
mapping. A continuous causal ideal-recovery diagnostic characterizes sensitivity; it is not a deployable
coding method. Here, active ticks are simulation steps, an event is a signed graded scalar amplitude, and
the opportunity budget bounds extra per-channel event slots rather than equalizing actual energy.
The empirical answer is bounded. In the tested strong-teacher setting, message objectives and fixed-
budget residual selection did not establish a robust structural advantage over local and counterfactual
controls. At MNIST T2, actual-message allocation was lower than baseline by 0.586 percentage points and
lower than local allocation by 0.977 points on paired means over 512 graphs; both descriptive intervals
crossed zero and quantify graph sampling only. At T1, message allocation reduced normalized actual
1

next-message MSE relative to local allocation on the reported MNIST and CIFAR10 validations, but this
representation-level change does not by itself imply improved classification. Thus, the results distinguish an
observable sensitivity or message-matching signal from a demonstrated classification benefit and do not
support a claim of a successful new conversion algorithm.
2
Related Work
ANN-to-SNN conversion uses calibration data and pretrained ANN activations to reduce mismatches
introduced by spiking dynamics.
Layerwise calibration and data-driven threshold or initial-potential
settings use activation information to configure conversion [1, 2]. Trainable neuron quantization studies
representational limits at low timesteps [3], while differential coding changes how temporal differences
encode activations [4]. Other work addresses temporal misalignment [5] or develops coarse-to-fine calibration
and coding strategies, including spiking language models [6] and single-spike phase coding, which has also
been applied to GCN conversion [7]. These methods provide distinct ways to manage temporal coding and
calibration. These coding and calibration precedents motivate the present conditional comparison of local
reconstruction and actual-operator message targets.
Another line of work adapts quantization to graph structure. Degree-sensitive masking and aggregation-
aware quantization account for node or aggregation properties when selecting quantization behavior [8, 9].
Adaptive calibration also considers firing patterns, sensitivity, and input-dependent temporal budgets [10].
This literature motivates studying graph-dependent errors. Our empirical question is narrower: whether
sensitivity-informed allocation of a finite event opportunity budget improves low-timestep converted GCN
classification beyond local reconstruction and counterfactual operators. We test this distinction directly,
without inferring it from graph-aware quantization results or comparisons across incompatible published
benchmarks. The GCN classification setting follows established graph benchmark and superpixel task
designs [11].

3.2
Calibration objectives and controls
Calibration changes initial membrane offsets while preserving the teacher weights, channel thresholds,
and hard event decisions in the forward pass. The common histogram thresholds were estimated on the
full training split before these screens. An offset is initialized to zero and parameterized as θρ, with ρ
clipped to [−0.5, 0.5]. Fitting all six sites uses 693 offsets; first-site-only fitting uses 146. We used the
same 512 fixed-index training calibration graphs, batch size 32, and 64 Adam updates for the screening
comparisons, with learning rates 0.001 and 0.01 treated as separate conditions. The backward pass uses a
ReLU membrane surrogate; it does not replace hard events in the forward computation.
The all-site local objective averages six sitewise mean-squared errors, each divided by the batchwise
teacher activation mean square with denominator floor 10−6. Let L0 be the first-site normalized local
error, L the six-site local mean, and Lm the normalized first-site next-message error. First-message fitting
minimizes (L0 + Lm)/2 with 146 offsets; all-message fitting minimizes (L + Lm)/2 with 693 offsets. Both
message variants target the first site’s immediate next message; the latter adds no later-site message
terms. If E is the converted-minus-teacher error at that site, the immediate preactivation perturbation

We also compared fixed source-percentile settings from the differential-coding implementation [4]. The
GCN comparator estimates per-channel 0.999 quantiles, floored at 10−6, from frozen post-ReLU teacher

reproduction of its original converter. The original image example uses four levels, threshold scale 4 and
32 ticks. We transfer its level/scale constants to T = 1 and T = 2 only as a configuration diagnostic. Its
512-graph threshold estimate also differs from the inherited full-training histogram, so this is not a fully
matched reproduction of the source pipeline. A second preset uses eight levels and threshold scale 1 to
match the local event alphabet; no validation-based scale search is used.
A separate T1 hard-offset diagnostic uses one sweep over 146 channel offsets and 33 fixed candidates per
coordinate on [−0.5, 0.5], retaining the incumbent when no hard-loss decrease is found. Its 4,818 candidate
evaluations are not budget-matched to 64 Adam updates. This coarse grid can miss small-offset minima
and does not establish a global capacity bound.

3.4
First-site alphabet and residual allocation
The diagnostic families change different resources: offset fits change 146 or 693 initial-state scalars, alphabet
fits change 1,168 first-site amplitudes, and allocation changes which nodes receive extra first-tick events
while preserving the fitted offsets. Comparisons within each family isolate its stated objective or control;
comparisons across families assess different capacities and fitting budgets. The alphabet-capacity probe
changes only the first site. It replaces the positive amplitude ladder with eight nonnegative channel-specific
values plus zero, using nearest-neighbor midpoint decisions while retaining signed differential emissions;
all later sites retain their original quantizer. Sixteen weighted Lloyd iterations fit 1,168 scalar values. A
separate hard-objective refinement starts from the same local-Lloyd solution and makes one 1,168-coordinate
sweep, considering step fractions 1, 0.5, and 0.25; an incumbent is retained unless the actual hard loss
decreases. This diagnostic has a larger fitting and storage budget than the 64-step Adam calibration and
the 146 first-site offsets. “Full-message” refinement minimizes the complete squared message objective,

3.5
Evaluation scope
Historical full experiments use teachers with seeds 41, 42, and 43. Official train/validation/test counts are
55,000/5,000/10,000 for MNIST and 45,000/5,000/10,000 for CIFAR10. Their test-set results describe the

512 graphs. Test data were not loaded for selecting the new methods. Many candidate families and settings

4.2
Calibration objectives and realizability
The teacher-41 T1 screen separates first-site targeting from the fitting objective (Table 2). With 146 offsets
and learning rate 0.001, first-local/message accuracy was 73.63/73.83% on MNIST and 45.51/43.95% on
CIFAR10. The corresponding 693-offset all-local/message values were 75.00/74.41% and 47.85/48.83%. The
higher-rate all-local control reached 78.13% and 49.41%. Thus first-site sensitivity did not translate into a
consistent advantage from first-site-only fitting or the message term. These are single-teacher validation
accuracies, not seed averages.
Hard training loss sometimes increased under the surrogate despite finite gradients and actual parameter
changes. A bounded hard-offset search was therefore used as an optimization diagnostic; it did not establish
a consistent actual-graph advantage over the feature and permuted controls. Its coarse grid and different
update budget limit the interpretation: failure of this search is not a proof that the parameter family has
no useful solution.

4.3
Fixed-opportunity residual allocation
Residual allocation was evaluated against baseline, random selection, local-gain selection, identity-operator
selection, and shuffled-operator selection, all using the same per-graph opportunity rule. The budget
provides 146 extra scalar slots per selected node and 146⌈n/4⌉per graph. Across the 512-graph validation
subset, this totals 1,344,222 slots over 9,207 selected nodes for MNIST and 2,225,186 slots over 15,241
selected nodes for CIFAR10. Actual message-policy nonzero extras were 612,232 and 926,039, respectively;
thus equal opportunity did not imply equal realized event counts or energy.
At T = 1, actual-message allocation reached 79.49% on MNIST and 49.22% on CIFAR10. Relative to
baseline, paired mean differences were +4.4922 percentage points on MNIST (descriptive graph-sampling
interval 1.1087 to 7.8757) and +1.3672 on CIFAR10 (−1.9932 to 4.7275). Relative to local allocation,
differences were +2.3438 points (−0.2472 to 4.9347) and +0.5859 (−2.3569 to 3.5288), respectively. Absolute
results for identity and shuffled-operator controls are reported in Table 3; paired contrasts with the controls,
including the stronger-local condition, are summarized in Figure 3. They do not establish a consistent
actual-operator advantage. These paired intervals describe graph sampling for one teacher and do not
establish seed-level reliability or generalization.
At T = 2, message allocation achieved 88.67% on MNIST and 53.32% on CIFAR10, versus baseline values
of 89.26% and 53.91%. Message-minus-baseline differences were −0.5859 points on both tasks (MNIST

points on MNIST (−2.4582 to 0.5051) and +0.9766 on CIFAR10 (−0.6011 to 2.5542). The direction varies

4.4
Source adaptation and diagnostic cost
The source-percentile transfer exposed a configuration-sensitive adverse case: the four-level, threshold-scale-4
image preset produced very low T = 1 accuracy on both tasks and remained weak at T = 2. With the
eight-level, scale-1 event-matched preset, CIFAR10 reached 46.29% at T = 1 and 55.08% at T = 2; MNIST
reached 71.88% and 88.09%. This supports treating the transferred image configuration as a diagnostic
rather than attributing its performance to graph structure. Alphabet probes likewise have a different
resource scope from the low-dimensional offset fits: first-site Lloyd fitting stores 1,168 scalars, and the
optional hard-objective sweep adds a full coordinate pass; neither is an all-site alphabet intervention. The
measured evidence therefore does not isolate a structural gain at matched capacity across these separate
probes.

5
Discussion
The results separate sensitivity to graph propagation from the ability to realize a useful correction with a
finite event code. Under the fixed folded graph convolution, the next-message perturbation is exactly the
graph operator applied to the activation error and then transformed by the next-layer weights. This makes
message error a well-defined local target for testing directional error. The continuous causal diagnostic shows
that some conversion errors are conditionally recoverable, particularly at node sites, but that intervention
can reproduce a continuous ReLU response and does not impose the finite alphabet, event dynamics, or
downstream classification constraints of the converted network. It therefore establishes a sensitivity property
of the studied computation, not an available low-timestep coding policy.
The membrane-calibration screen did not establish a consistent advantage for the actual-message objective
over local fitting. Separately, actual-message residual allocation reduced its targeted immediate message
error, but this did not produce robust classification gains. The message objective covers the immediate
linear propagation term; it is a proxy for that term, not an exact loss for the residual block or final
prediction. Subsequent nonlinearities and errors at other sites remain in the computation. These results
are consistent with a mismatch between local message fidelity and classification, but they do not identify
which downstream component accounts for the observed differences.
The fixed-opportunity allocation experiment provides a direct test of whether using the actual graph
mapping adds value beyond selecting residual corrections. At the shortest prefix, message allocation
sometimes improved on baseline, most clearly for MNIST, but its paired comparisons with local and
counterfactual operator controls were inconclusive across the two datasets. At two active ticks, the direction
of the differences varied by dataset and comparator, with the reported graph-sampling intervals crossing zero.
Thus, the observed short-prefix gains do not establish a robust contribution from the actual graph structure.
The controls are informative because a ranking based on the actual operator can appear graph-aware while
10

gains may also arise from residual selection or the allocation procedure itself. In this study, the comparisons
do not distinguish a dependable actual-operator advantage from those alternatives.
The opportunity budget also limits what can be inferred about resource efficiency. It bounds extra
per-channel event slots but does not equalize the number of nonzero corrections or their energy across
policies. Consequently, accuracy comparisons under this rule assess policies with matched opportunities, not
matched realized activity or energy. The measured allocation path incurred additional host time under the
reported implementation and timing boundary. This is a diagnostic cost for the tested procedure; it does
not establish an optimized deployment cost, hardware energy, or a general runtime penalty for graph-aware
methods.
Capacity and configuration further constrain interpretation. The adverse transferred source-percentile
configuration shows that a preset developed for another setting need not be suitable for these graph models.
The event-matched preset and the larger first-site alphabet probes use different coding or fitting resources
from the offset calibrations, so their results do not isolate a structural effect at matched capacity. More
broadly, the experiments concern one strong teacher for the new method screens and a fixed subset of
previously exposed validation data; the paired intervals describe graph sampling for that teacher, not
seed variation or external generalization. The evidence supports a bounded empirical conclusion about
these tested methods and conditions. It leaves reliability across teachers and independent tasks, complete
source-converter comparisons, and the novelty and breadth required for a method contribution unresolved.
6
Conclusion
In the tested low-timestep GCN-to-SNN setting, critical-site sensitivity did not translate into an established,
realizable graph-specific classification advantage. Actual-message residual allocation improved its targeted
immediate message error, but neither the membrane-calibration screen nor fixed-opportunity allocation
established robust classification superiority over local and counterfactual controls across datasets and
active-tick settings. Continuous ideal recovery identifies conditional sensitivity, while finite-event calibration
and allocation remain constrained by coding capacity and by the fact that immediate message error is only
a proxy for final prediction. These findings do not support a claim of a successful new conversion method;
they show that sensitivity and message matching alone are insufficient evidence for a practical structural
benefit in the evaluated setting.
